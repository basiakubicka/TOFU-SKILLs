# Surprising-Number Hook Template Bank

8 hook templates extracted from the golden dataset (20 real, high-performing
surprising-number / stat-shock posts in this skill's a golden set of real high-performing posts, likes
117–6,197). Each is a **distinct rhetorical mechanism** with **fixed, literal words**
locking the rhythm in place, not a vibe description or a reskin of the same sentence.
Pick the template that fits the shape of the number you actually have. Don't default to
template 1 (single-shock stat) just because it's the archetype's signature move; the
split-percentage and stacked-stat mechanisms overperform it at the top of the dataset.

This is a reference for hook VARIETY. Do not copy an example hook verbatim; fill the
template with the specifics of the number you're reporting.

**Format rule for every slot:** the bracket itself carries the specific description,
never a generic label like `[SLOT A]`. Write `[the single surprising dollar figure,
stated to its real precision]`, not `[X]` with the description in a separate paragraph.
A drafter must be able to fill the slot correctly from the bracket text alone, without
cross-referencing anything else.

Every template has:
- **Fixed template**: the literal words, verbatim, with descriptive `[bracketed]` slots
  inline. Read the fixed words alone (skip the brackets) and they form a recognizable,
  repeatable rhythm on their own.
- **Slot test**: for slots needing more than the bracket can hold, a concrete pass/fail
  check (a real test, not more vague description).
- **Why it works**: the psychological/rhetorical mechanism, secondary to the fixed words.
- **Real example(s)**: the actual post(s) the template was extracted from, with attribution.
- **Use when / non-negotiables**: situational fit, plus any hard rule.

**The hard limits apply to every template below:** hook line 1 ≤ 62 characters, line 2 ≤
50 characters, no unicode-bold in the hook. Count the characters before finalizing. Note:
two dataset posts (Adam Danyal, Chorouk Malmoum) lead with a bold-unicode newsletter ad
or a 🚨 emoji block before their real hook — that ad/alert line is not part of the
template, only their true first content line counts.

**The one non-negotiable underneath every template in this bank:** the headline number
must be REAL, CHECKABLE, and stated to its true precision. This archetype's entire
mechanism is a reader thinking "that can't be right" and then verifying it. A rounded,
inflated, or unsourced number breaks the mechanism on first inspection. Never fabricate a
number to make a hook land — if you don't have a real one, you don't have this archetype.

### 1. Single-Shock Stat
**Fixed template:** "[The one surprising number, stated flat to its real precision: a
dollar figure, a count, a duration].
[The one line that makes it impossible to ignore: the twist, the contrast, or the
defense — "They paid $200." / "this is not a typo."]"
**Slot test for the number:** it must be a single, specific, checkable figure that a
stranger would find implausible on sight ("$27,000 of compute in 23 days," "98.7%
accuracy"). A round or expected number ("thousands of dollars," "high accuracy") fails —
the shock is in the precision.
**Why it works:** one implausible number with no cushion forces a double-take, and line 2
(the twist) opens the loop the body has to close. The reader can't resolve "how is that
possible?" without reading on.
**Real examples:** *"One Claude user consumed $27,000 of compute in 23 days. / They paid
$200. Now everyone's limits are getting cut."* (Alex Banks, 2,696 likes) · *"98.7%
accuracy with zero vectors; this is not a typo."* (Rakesh Gohel, 2,009 likes)
**Use when:** you have one genuinely startling, precise figure and a one-line twist that
sharpens it. If the number needs a paragraph of setup to seem surprising, it isn't a
single-shock stat — use template 4 or 5.

### 2. Split-Percentage / The Math
**Fixed template:** "[The reframing label: "The uncomfortable math of [the thing]:"]
[Small %] is [the part everyone focuses on]. [Large %] is [the part nobody budgets for /
nobody talks about]."
**Slot test for the split:** the two percentages must sum sensibly (10/90, 20/80) and the
smaller share must be the part the audience overrates while the larger is the part they
ignore. If both halves are things people already weight correctly, there's no reframe.
**Why it works:** a lopsided ratio reframes a whole belief in one line — it tells the
reader they've been paying attention to the wrong 10%. The math feels objective, which
lands harder than an opinion would.
**Real example:** *"The uncomfortable math of AI transformation: 10% is the model. / 90%
is work your organization hasn't budgeted for."* (Andreas Horn, 1,402 likes)
**Use when:** the real insight is a proportion the audience has backwards (they credit the
flashy minority share, ignore the consequential majority). The percentages should be
defensible estimates, not invented precision — if you can't back the split, say "most" vs
"a fraction" instead of faking a number.

### 3. Cost-Contrast / Two Numbers Side by Side
**Fixed template:** "[The first number, usually the shocking-high one] vs [the second
number, the humbling-low one] for [the thing/person being compared].
[The dry line that lets the gap speak: "Wild how that math works out."]"
**Slot test for the two numbers:** both must be real and directly comparable (same unit,
same period), and the GAP between them must be the surprise, not either number alone.
"$150K/month vs $4.5K" passes because the 33x gap is the point; two similar numbers fail.
**Why it works:** putting the two figures on screen together does the argument before the
reader reaches the body — the disparity is self-evident, and the flat closing line
refuses to over-explain it, which reads as confidence.
**Real example:** *"$150K/month in tokens vs $4.5K for Rohan. / Wild how that math works
out."* (Eduardo Ordax, 6,197 likes — top of the entire dataset)
**Use when:** the insight is a disparity between two real costs/outcomes. The two numbers
must be genuinely comparable — a misleading apples-to-oranges contrast gets called out in
the comments and sinks the post's credibility.

### 4. Stacked-Stat Gap
**Fixed template:** "[Stat 1: the high adoption/optimism number]. [Stat 2: the much lower
success/value number]. [Optional Stat 3, sharper still: the near-zero real-impact number].
[The one line naming the gap those stats reveal.]"
**Slot test for the stack:** each stat must come from a nameable source (McKinsey, MIT,
BCG, a named survey) and the sequence must descend into a widening gap — high adoption,
low value, near-zero impact. Three unrelated stats that don't build to one gap fail; this
is a funnel, not a stat dump.
**Why it works:** each number narrows the reader's sense of the field's real progress, and
by the third stat the gap is undeniable and self-assembled — the reader concludes "so
almost nobody's actually winning" before you say it.
**Real example:** *"88% of organizations now use AI. / Only 5% capture value at scale. 95%
of pilots show no measurable P&L impact."* (Michael Lee, 1,348 likes — note: line 2 runs
long in the post; for the hook, keep L2 ≤ 50 and push the third stat into the body)
**Use when:** you have 2-3 real, sourced stats that descend into a gap (adoption high,
outcomes low). Every stat must be attributable — this template is the most source-hungry
in the bank, and one unsourced number contaminates the whole stack.

### 5. Big-Outcome Transformation
**Fixed template:** "[Named company] [did the dramatic-sounding thing with a number: "replaced 8,500 roles with AI"].
And [the surprising positive/negative outcome, with its own number: "the company is now $1.4 billion richer"]."
**Slot test for both numbers:** the input number (what they did) and the output number
(what happened) must both be real and cited to the company/source, and the twist is that
the outcome contradicts the expectation the first number sets ("replaced roles" →
expectation is harm; "$1.4B richer, retrained everyone" → surprise).
**Why it works:** the first number sets an expectation (usually alarming), the second
number subverts it, and the reader reads the body to find out how a scary input produced a
surprising output. The named company makes it checkable and shareable.
**Real examples:** *"IKEA replaced 8,500 customer service roles with AI. / And the company
is now $1.4 billion richer because of it..."* (Chris Donnelly, 2,931 likes) · *"IKEA...
turned 8,500 displaced roles into a $1.4B interior design business by reskilling their
people."* (Allie K. Miller, 1,872 likes — same event, two framings, both performed)
**Use when:** a named organization did something with a number, and the outcome (also a
number) subverts the obvious expectation. Requires a real, named, verifiable case — this
is the least fabricable template, which is exactly why it travels.

### 6. Counterintuitive-Finding Question
**Fixed template:** "[The surprising research finding, posed as near-disbelief: "People are
kinder to LLMs than to each other??"]
Here's what the research [actually] says..."
**Slot test for the finding:** it must be a real, sourced result (a named survey, study,
or dataset) that genuinely contradicts intuition, phrased as the reader's own "wait,
what?" reaction. A finding people already expect ("AI is getting better") fails — the
question mark has to be earned by real surprise.
**Why it works:** posing the surprising result as a question (with the doubled "??")
mirrors the reader's own disbelief, and the "here's what the research says" promise makes
the payoff feel like resolution rather than a claim. It invites the reader to check.
**Real example:** *"People are kinder to LLMs than to each other?? / Here's what the
research says..."* (GenAI Works, 4,297 likes)
**Use when:** you have a genuinely counterintuitive, sourced finding (usually from a survey
or study) and want the hook to voice the reader's disbelief. The body MUST deliver the real
numbers behind the finding (the 70%, the 12%) — a question with no data behind it reads as
clickbait and the archetype collapses.

### 7. High-Stakes Number
**Fixed template:** "[The number that names what's at stake, stated flat: "$100K AI
projects fail." / "$50M was at risk."]
[The line that reframes WHY, rejecting the obvious cause: "Not because of bad models.
Because of missing layers."]"
**Slot test for the stakes number:** it must be a real, representative figure for the
stakes involved (a typical project cost, a real amount at risk), and line 2 must reject the
obvious explanation and point at the real one. A stakes number with no reframe is just
alarming, not insightful.
**Why it works:** leading with the money at stake creates immediate weight, and rejecting
the obvious cause in line 2 opens a "then why?" loop that the body's real explanation
closes. The number makes the abstract lesson feel expensive and urgent.
**Example shape:** *"[$BIG_NUMBER things fail]. / Not because of [the obvious cause]. Because of [the real, overlooked cause]."* (pairs a stakes number with an immediate cause-reframe, then decodes it in the body — swap in one of your own high-performers)
**Use when:** the point is a lesson about failure/risk and a number makes the stakes
concrete. Keep the number honest and representative — don't invent a scary figure to raise
the stakes. Guard against drift into MOFU: if the number sets up a first-person story with
a turning point ("$50M was at risk. We had 5 days."), that's a personal narrative, route it
to a MOFU (trust) post; out of scope for this TOFU pack.

### 8. Opportunity Number
**Fixed template:** "[The named source] will pay you [the surprising amount] to [do the
thing] for [the timeframe]. [Optional: "No experience needed."]
[The line that makes it feel real, not too-good-to-be-true: the credible detail.]"
**Slot test for the amount:** the figure and every term (duration, stipend, requirements)
must be real and current, pulled from the actual announcement — this template reports an
opportunity, so an inflated or stale number is a factual error, not a rhetorical flourish.
**Why it works:** a large, specific, low-barrier number ("$15,400/month... no experience
needed") triggers both desire and disbelief, and the reader reads the body to find the
catch. It's the most saved-and-shared shape because readers tag people it could help.
**Real examples:** *"Anthropic will pay you $15,400/month to join their Fellows Program for
4 months. No experience needed!"* (Chorouk Malmoum, 1,412 likes) · *"Anthropic launched a
4-month Fellows Program. Get paid $3,850/week to break into AI safety research."* (Stanislav
Beliaev, 1,212 likes — same program, two framings, both performed)
**Use when:** you're reporting a real, current opportunity with a surprising number and a
low barrier. Every term must be verifiable against the source announcement — this is a
reporting template, and getting the number or the requirements wrong is a credibility
failure, not a style miss.

## Explicitly excluded from this bank
Mechanisms that appeared in the dataset but belong to a sibling archetype, or overlapped
too closely with a template above, are intentionally left out rather than padded in:
- **Priced-plan downgrade** ("$30/month for Copilot... using it like a spell checker" —
  Gabriel Millien): the number is a foil for a shallow-use callout, and the payload is a
  runnable setup. That's Tactical How-To (`tofu-howto`, template 1/2), not stat-shock.
- **Percent-of-power-left-on-the-table** ("80% of its power on the table," "use 10% of it"
  — Chorouk Malmoum, Gabriel Millien): the number is a hook wrapper on a how-to breakdown of
  a tool's features. Route to `tofu-howto` (template 8, plain how-to authority).
- **Stakes-number-into-personal-story** ("$50M was at risk. We had 5 days to save it." —
  Stephanie Hills): a real first-person arc with a cost and a turning point. That's MOFU
  (a MOFU/trust post, out of scope here), not a universal stat-shock. See template 7's guard note.

## Verifying the headline number (never assume it)
Every template in this bank rests on one real, checkable number. Before using: confirm the
figure against its actual source (the announcement, the study, the price sheet, the
benchmark) and state it to its true precision. If you can't source it, flag it as
unverified rather than posting it bare — an unsourced surprising number is the single
fastest way to lose this archetype's trust mechanism.

## How to use this bank when drafting
1. Identify the shape of the number you have: one implausible figure (→ 1), a lopsided
   ratio (→ 2), two comparable costs (→ 3), a descending stack of stats (→ 4), a named
   company's in-out transformation (→ 5), a counterintuitive research finding (→ 6), a
   stakes figure with a cause-reframe (→ 7), or a real low-barrier opportunity (→ 8).
   Match the template to the number you actually have.
2. Fill every bracket with something **specific and real**: the exact dollar figure, the
   real percentage split, the named source. If a slot can't be filled with a real number,
   don't fabricate it — pick a different template or track down the missing figure.
3. Check the "why it works" line. If your draft hook doesn't create that mechanism (the
   double-take, the self-assembling gap, the subverted expectation), it isn't using the
   template correctly even if it superficially matches the words.
4. **Run the slot-substitution self-test before finalizing:** read only the fixed words
   (skip the brackets). Do they form a recognizable rhythm on their own? If a bracket says
   only `[X]` with no description inline, it's not filled in — write the specific
   description directly into the bracket.
5. **Count the characters.** Line 1 ≤ 62, line 2 ≤ 50, no unicode-bold in the hook. No
   exceptions.
