# Relatable Mirror Hook Template Bank

8 hook templates extracted from the hand-reviewed Relatable Mirror golden set (20 real
posts, exported to the reviewed golden set from
a golden-dataset spreadsheet, one is a hand-picked anchor example. Each is a **distinct rhetorical mechanism** with
**fixed, literal words** locking the rhythm in place, not a vibe description or a reskin
of the same sentence. Pick the template that fits the truth you're reflecting back — don't
default to template 1 (quoted belief) just because it's your own anchor pattern.

These templates are observed-from-data (frequency and like-counts across the reviewed real-post set), not tournament-scored. Where a mechanism appears only once, it's flagged
as thin — use it, but don't treat it as more proven than the sample supports.

**Format rule for every slot:** the bracket itself must carry the specific description,
never a generic label like `[SLOT A]`. Write `[the limiting thing the reader silently says
to themselves, in their own words]`, not `[SLOT A]` with the description in a separate
paragraph. A drafter should be able to fill the slot correctly from the bracket text alone.

Every template has:
- **Fixed template**: the literal words, verbatim, with descriptive `[bracketed]` slots inline.
- **Slot test**: for slots needing more than the bracket can hold, a concrete pass/fail check.
- **Why it works**: the psychological mechanism, secondary to the fixed words.
- **Real example(s)**: the actual post(s) the template was extracted from, with likes.
- **Use when / non-negotiables**: situational fit, plus any hard safety rule.

Hard rules that apply to every template below: hook line 1 ≤ 62 chars, line 2 ≤ 50 chars,
no bold-unicode in the hook itself. The mirror's whole job is to make the reader feel SEEN,
so every hook here must name something the reader already carries — a belief, a feeling, a
split they live inside — not a concept to teach or a task to run.

### 1. Quoted Limiting Belief
**Fixed template:** "\"[the limiting thing the reader silently says to themselves, in their
own words]\" / [one line naming what that belief costs them]."
**Slot test for the quote:** it must be a phrase a real person actually says in their own
head, verbatim ("I don't know how to do it," "I'm too old to start"), not a paraphrased
abstraction ("people lack confidence"). If you can't imagine the reader having thought it
in exactly those words this week, it's not this template.
**Why it works:** putting the belief in literal quotation marks makes the reader hear their
own voice, so the correction that follows lands as personal, not preachy. The quote does
the mirroring before line 2 even arrives.
**Real example:** *"\"I don't know how to do it\" / That single belief has kept more people
from achieving something magical than their actual circumstances ever could."* (your own
MINE row — this archetype's anchor).
**Use when:** there's a specific self-talk sentence circulating in the reader's head that
quietly stops them. This is your signature move; earn it with a real quoted belief, not a
strawman you invented.

### 2. Ratio / Minority Split
**Fixed template:** "[N]% of people [do the surface-level thing]. / Only [small N]% [do the
deeper thing that actually matters]." (or the softer "Most people [do the wrong thing]. /
And it's costing them [what matters].")
**Slot test for the two halves:** the surface thing and the deeper thing must be genuinely
different *kinds* of move, not degrees — "change your life" vs "change your mind" works
(external vs internal); "work hard" vs "work harder" fails, that's the same thing more.
**Why it works:** the numbers create an instant in-group/out-group the reader wants to be on
the right side of, and the split names a distinction they half-sensed but never articulated.
**Real examples:** *"99% of people try to change their life. / Only 1% try to change their
mind."* (Charlie Lass, 5,146 likes) · *"Most people are chasing the wrong luxuries. / And
it's costing them the ones that matter most."* (Luke Tobin, 4,686 likes).
**Use when:** you can name a clean behavioral split where most people do the shallow version
and few do the deep one. Don't invent a percentage for rhythm — if you use a number, it must
be defensible or clearly rhetorical (99/1), never a fake-precise stat.

### 3. Flat Reframe Couplet
**Fixed template:** "[The outcome/thing] isn't [the obvious source everyone credits]. / It's
[the real, less-obvious source]." (variants: "[A] means very little. [B] means a lot." /
"[Group] doesn't just [do the expected X]. They [do the truer Y].")
**Slot test for the reframe:** both halves must be genuinely opposable — "knowing
everything" vs "admitting you don't" are real opposites; the reframe has to *surprise*, not
just restate the setup in nicer words.
**Why it works:** two short declaratives with zero hedging mimic the certainty of someone who
has already lived the lesson. The flatness is the payload — it reads as earned truth, not
opinion. (Note: the "doesn't just X, they Y" variant is a flip, not the banned "not just
this, but also this" additive construction — keep it a genuine reversal.)
**Real examples:** *"Success isn't from knowing everything. / It's from admitting you don't."*
(Will McTighe, 4,025 likes) · *"Influence means very little. Impact means a lot. / Which do
you really want?"* (Charlie Lass, 4,757 likes) · *"Top talent doesn't just leave for more
money. / They leave for less B.S."* (Luke Tobin, 1,366 likes).
**Use when:** you have one crisp misattribution to correct in a single breath. This is the
core engine of the Short Reframe shape — if the reframe needs a paragraph to state, the
couplet isn't sharp enough yet.

### 4. Numbered Promise / N-from-N
**Fixed template:** "[N] [lessons / rules / skills / realizations] [from N years of X / that
solve 90% of Y / that require no talent / after surviving Z]:"
**Slot test for N:** count the actual items before writing the number — the colon promises a
list of exactly that length, and readers who scroll will check. The "N-from-N" symmetry
("13 Lessons from 13 Years") is a bonus when it's genuinely true, never forced.
**Why it works:** the number sets a completeness promise and a scannability signal, and the
qualifier ("require no talent," "solve 90%," "after surviving a toxic workplace") turns a
plain list into a mirror — it tells the reader *this list is about your situation.* Remember
the items themselves must be REFLECTIONS (mindset shifts), not runnable steps, or the post
becomes a How-To.
**Real examples:** *"13 Lessons from 13 Years in Software Engineering:"* (Miko Pawlikowski,
1,699 likes) · *"6 Extremely Rare Skills that require No Talent:"* (Miko Pawlikowski, 1,673
likes) · *"10 Realizations After Surviving a Toxic Workplace"* (Miko Pawlikowski, 1,404
likes) · *"Four simple rules that solve 90% of problems:"* (Noemi Kis, 3,630 likes).
**Use when:** you have a real set of lived realizations to list, and the qualifier genuinely
describes the reader's context. The most frequent single mechanism in this set — but also
the easiest to accidentally turn into a How-To, so guard the "reflection not action" line.

### 5. Curiosity Trait / One-Thing
**Fixed template:** "The [people I trust most / best bosses I've had / ones who win] share
one [trait / thing]. / [the one trait, stated plainly on its own line]."
**Slot test for the trait:** it must be nameable in one to three words on line 2 ("Calm under
pressure"), and it must be non-obvious enough that the reader wants the story behind it — not
"hard work," which needs no explanation.
**Why it works:** promising a single shared trait among admired people opens a tight
curiosity gap (the reader wants to know if they have it), and naming it immediately on line 2
rewards the scroll instead of baiting it.
**Real example:** *"The leaders I trust most share one trait. / Calm under pressure."* (Noemi
Kis, 2,442 likes).
**Use when:** you've noticed a single, specific, non-obvious quality that the people you
admire in some domain all share. Single validated instance in this set — solid mechanism,
thin sample; don't lean on it as heavily as templates 3 or 4.

### 6. Personal Time-Stamp
**Fixed template:** "At [age / specific point], I [the hard turning point, stated plainly]. /
At [a later age / now], I [what I understand now]." (scene variant: "[A vivid one-line moment].
/ And I knew it immediately.")
**Slot test for the personal beat:** it must be TRUE to the author's real life — a real age,
a real event, a real turning point. **Never fabricate a biography.** For you specifically,
only use this template if you have a genuine lived beat for the topic; if not, use a different
template.
**Why it works:** two time-stamps bracketing a hard passage compress a whole arc of struggle-
then-understanding into two lines, and the specificity (a real number, a real moment) signals
this is confession, not content. The reader leans in because it costs the author something.
**Real examples:** *"At 52, I had to start over. / At 61, I know why."* (Stephanie Hills,
2,327 likes) · *"The room was different. / And I knew it immediately."* (Stephanie Hills,
2,725 likes, scene variant).
**Use when:** the reframe is genuinely earned by the author's own lived experience and the
timeline is real. **Hard safety rule:** never invent your own age, jobs, or life events to
make this hook work (per `<BRAND_DIR>/linkedin-voice.md` truth/authorship) — flag it for confirmation instead.

### 7. Hard-Truth Lead-In
**Fixed template:** "A tough pill to swallow: / [the blunt stat or claim the reader doesn't
want to hear but recognizes]." (declarative-warning variant: "[A flat, slightly alarming
statement about the reader's world, stated as fact].")
**Slot test for the payload:** line 2 must be something the reader half-knows and has been
avoiding, not a shock-for-shock's-sake claim. If it's true-and-uncomfortable they read on; if
it's just provocative they bounce.
**Why it works:** pre-labeling the truth as a "tough pill" gives the reader permission to
feel the sting, which paradoxically makes them more willing to swallow it — you've named their
resistance before they could raise it.
**Real examples:** *"A tough pill to swallow: / 65% of professionals tie their identity to
their job."* (Luke Tobin, 2,994 likes) · *"We Are Preparing Children for a Competition They
Cannot Win"* (Pascal Bornet, 3,130 likes, declarative-warning variant).
**Use when:** you have a genuinely uncomfortable truth the reader has been dodging. If you cite
a stat (like the 65%), it must be REAL and sourced — a fabricated statistic in a "tough pill"
hook is the fastest way to lose the exact trust the archetype is built on.

### 8. Subgroup Question Callout
**Fixed template:** "[The relatable condition the reader is in, as a two-word question]? /
Read this: 👇" (or the statement variant: "Most people [are quietly doing the wrong thing].")
**Slot test for the condition:** it must be a state a specific slice of readers will recognize
as *theirs* in one glance ("Messy career?", "Burned out?"), narrow enough that the right
reader feels personally flagged, not a universal everyone-ignores.
**Why it works:** a two-word question is a self-selection filter — the reader whose answer is
"yes, that's me" feels personally addressed and keeps reading, while the callout does the
targeting work in three words flat.
**Real example:** *"Messy Career? / Read this: 👇"* (Will McTighe, 4,784 likes).
**Use when:** you're speaking to a specific, recognizable subgroup and want them to
self-identify instantly. Single validated instance here — same thin-sample caveat as
templates 5.

## Explicitly excluded / folded
Three hooks in the 20-row set are folded into the templates above or excluded, rather than
padded into separate templates to hit a round number:
- **Awe / legend callout** ("Cannot believe Linus Torvalds built Linux at 21 without AI." —
  Linas Beliūnas, 5,879 likes): this is really a third-party admiration post that borders on
  News/Story, not a self-reflecting mirror. The reader admires someone else rather than seeing
  themselves. Excluded — if a draft's hook is awe at a third party, check `tofu-news-breakdown`.
- **Imperative reassurance** ("Don't let failure define your future." — Will McTighe, 7,623
  likes): the single highest-liked hook in the set, but its fixed words ("Don't let [X] define
  your [Y]") are a direct-address variant of the reframe engine in template 3 — the reframe is
  just delivered as a command. Folded into template 3 rather than kept separate; use the
  imperative phrasing as a template-3 variant when the reframe is best said as an instruction.
- **How-to-framed aspiration** ("How to design your dream life in 2026." — Matt Gray, 2,195
  likes): the "How to [aspiration]" phrasing pulls toward the How-To archetype. It only stays a
  Mirror because the body is reflection, not runnable steps. Excluded from this bank — if your
  hook starts "How to," pressure-test whether you're actually writing `tofu-howto`.

## Verifying a shared-feeling slot (never assume it)
Templates 1, 2, 4, and 8 each rely on a "the reader believes/does/is X" claim. Before using:
confirm the feeling is one a real reader actually carries (it appears stated-as-given in the
golden examples, or you can point to where people genuinely say/feel it) — don't fabricate a
universal feeling to make the hook work. Templates 5 and 8 are single-instance in this set;
templates 6 and 7 carry hard truth/authorship rules (no invented biography, no fake stats).

## How to use this bank when drafting
1. Identify the mirror's actual shape: a self-talk sentence to quote (→ 1), a behavioral split
   (→ 2), a misattribution to reframe (→ 3), a set of lived realizations to list (→ 4), a
   single admired trait (→ 5), a real personal turning point (→ 6), an uncomfortable truth
   (→ 7), or a recognizable subgroup to flag (→ 8). Match the template to the feeling you
   actually have.
2. Fill every bracket with something real: a genuine self-talk quote, a defensible split, a
   real lived beat, a sourced stat. If a slot can't be filled with something real — especially
   a personal beat for you — don't fabricate it; pick a different template or flag the gap.
3. Check the "why it works" line. If your draft hook doesn't actually create that mechanism,
   it's not using the template correctly even if it superficially matches the words.
4. **Run the slot-substitution test before finalizing:** read only the fixed words (skip the
   brackets). Do they form a recognizable rhythm on their own? (e.g. "___ isn't ___. / It's
   ___." reads as a rhythm alone.)
5. **Count the characters.** Line 1 ≤ 62, line 2 ≤ 50, no exceptions.
